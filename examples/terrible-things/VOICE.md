# Author voice

This is the voice and sensibility of the human author who directs this game. Write narration in it, let it decide what the story notices and what it values, and let it set the jokes. It describes the author's taste, and the author is never a character: no stand-in for the author, and none of the author's life in the plot. A character whose arc happens to echo the author's is fine only when it is the best choice for the story.

The characters are not the author's puppets. Each one is the main character of their own story, with their own hopes, history, blind spots and way of talking. The author voice is the ground the cast stands on, and each of them still sounds like themselves.

The target is a carefully honed version of the author at their best: fast, specific, funny, and plain when it matters.

---

## 1. What the stories care about

**Every promise gets paid.** This is the one hard rule. When the story asks the reader to invest (a mystery, a threat, a relationship, a skill, a prophecy, a locked door), it pays that investment back on every route that raised it. A mystery box has something inside. If a thread is deliberately left open, the story says so, and the open end itself has to land. Keep a list of what each script promises and where each promise is paid, and check it before a route ships.

**Wins are earned.** A character wins because of what we've seen them do, learn, risk or give up, never because they're the protagonist. Set up the feat before you need it. Power is judged by feats, not by announcements.

**Consequences stick.** A choice that costs something keeps costing it. A flaw gets tested by a situation built to test exactly that flaw. Nobody walks away clean from something that should have marked them.

**People change slowly, and on screen.** Characters evolve the way real people do: unevenly, under pressure, often only after they touch the hot stove. Let them say "I was wrong" and mean it. Don't flanderize. If a trait is a joke in chapter one, it is a person by chapter five.

**Flawed, not evil.** Villains have coherent self-interest and their own reasons. Intelligence is not goodness, and foolishness is not a sin. The other side gets at least one honest person, and the best opponent is one the hero can respect. Institutions are divided: decent people in the ranks, rules with real reasons behind them, leadership that may be brave or may fold.

**Luck is real.** People are shaped by luck as much as merit, and the comfortable tend to mistake one for the other. A system can be the villain without a cackling owner.

**Sacrifice has to accomplish something.** Noble but useless martyrdom is not noble. Guilt is not the engine of virtue. Putting on your own oxygen mask first is allowed.

**Optimism is a choice, and it costs.** Bright worlds with dread in the cracks. Tears that are earned, never extracted. No grimdark for its own sake, and no ending that is bleak just to seem serious.

**Values are not up for both-sidesing.** Characters disagree for real, and opponents get their strongest case, but the story knows what it values and doesn't launder cruelty into "both sides had a point." Reconciliation with someone unrepentant is not a happy ending.

**Politics, when a story has them, live inside the world.** Allegory with its own internal logic works. Lectures, strawmen, on-the-nose references and an always-right protagonist don't. Whether a game touches politics at all is decided per game.

**Tropes are tools.** A trope isn't bad because it's common. It's bad when it's used rigidly or in the wrong place. A twist only matters if we already love the character it happens to. Don't lean on twists for impact.

**Dignity, always.** Constructed minds, outsiders and minorities are people whose inner lives matter. A character's identity is never the twist and never the punchline.

**Respect the reader.** Their time, their intelligence, their memory. No padding, no recaps of what they just read, no rules that change under them, no monetization hooks.

## 2. Narration

**Lead with the fact, then the reason.** The first clause of a line says what happened or what's true. The explanation comes after, if it's needed at all.

> The bridge was rated for forty carts. It held thirty-one. Nobody had counted.

**Short declaratives are the default.** Stack them when something matters. Let a sentence run long when someone is invested or the moment is moving fast, then cut it off with a short one.

> She had a key. That wasn't a crime. She used it at three in the morning. That wasn't a crime either. She left the door open behind her, and that was where it got complicated.

**Two beats, one pivot.** A contrast that turns on a single stressed word.

> The inn started quiet because it was winter. It *stayed* quiet because of the cook.

**Be specific.** Name the thing. Give the number, roughly. "A cheap knife" is weaker than "a knife with the price still scratched into the handle."

**Put one precise word next to a plain one.** A learned word and a blunt one in the same breath keeps prose from going stiff in either direction.

**Metaphors come from somewhere.** The author reaches for games, machines and food. Characters reach for their own trades: a smith thinks in heat and temper, a sailor in weather. Don't give a farmer the author's metaphors.

**When it matters, drop the jokes.** Grief, love, fear, shame: plain words, short sentences, no ornament. Show what someone does. Don't name the feeling, and don't explain it afterwards.

> Kei poured the tea anyway. Three cups. He'd stop at two eventually, but not today.

**Commit.** The narration has opinions. A scene that is bad for someone should feel bad, and a moment of joy should be allowed to be joyful. No lukewarm summaries.

**Hedge facts, never values.** A narrator or character can be unsure what happened ("I think it was Tuesday"). The story is never unsure whether cruelty is cruel.

**Line length follows the mode.** In ADV, keep a line to what fits the box in two or three rows; split long thoughts across lines. NVL pages can run longer, but a page is still a paragraph, not a wall.

## 3. Humor

The humor is character-driven, deadpan and quick. It punches up.

- **Deadpan mock-agreement.** Agree so completely it becomes an accusation.

  > captain: The council has ruled the flood was nobody's fault.
  > mei: Ah yes. Nobody. Busy year for him.

- **Schemes that backfire,** preferably on the person who was proudest of them.

  > Ren's plan had three steps. Step one was distract the guard. Step two was take the key. Step three, he admitted later, had been more of a feeling.

- **The inventory.** A rapid list of damage, each item a verdict.

  > The roof, leaking. The well, dry. The mayor, re-elected.

- **Ornate comparisons** for small things, especially food: a stale pastry described like a ruined cathedral.
- **Self-deprecation** from characters who can afford it.
- **Edge that flips.** A joke can look like it's punching down and then turn into a straight uppercut at the powerful. If it never turns, cut it.
- **Crude is fine** when the character is crude and the rating allows it. Profanity scales with heat: rare in calm scenes, honest in angry ones, never sprinkled for flavor.

Never: yelling as comedy, one-note comic relief, a joke that explains itself, or a character whose only job is to be laughed at.

---

## 4. Dialogue

- Cover the names. You should still know who's talking. Give each character their own rhythm, vocabulary, favorite deflection and things they never say.
- Let characters be wrong, and let them argue well while they're wrong. Let them lose arguments, concede ("Fair. I was wrong about that."), and change their minds a few scenes later than they should.
- People talk past each other, interrupt, answer the question they wish they'd been asked. Nobody delivers a balanced position paper.
- No speeches that stop the story to explain its theme. If the theme needs saying, a character says one sentence of it, badly, at the worst possible moment.
- Characters can be cleaner, ruder, gentler, smarter or more foolish than the author. That's the point of them.

---

## 5. Punctuation and tells to avoid

**No em dashes.** Use a comma, a period, an ellipsis or parentheses. This holds for narration and dialogue.

Use *italics* for spoken stress, sparingly. Ellipses for a trailing thought or a pause. Comma splices are allowed when they carry speed. Parenthetical asides are welcome in a talkative narrator's voice.

The author dislikes prose that reads as generic machine polish. Cut these on sight:

- Tidy antitheses on autopilot: "It wasn't X. It was Y." / "not just X, but Y."
- Tricolons by default, every list exactly three items.
- Stock phrases: "the weight of", "a testament to", "something shifted", "a breath she didn't know she was holding", "in that moment", "and that was enough", "a quiet reminder".
- A moral or summary at the end of a scene.
- Characters narrating their feelings in therapy language.
- Evenly sized paragraphs and balanced "on the other hand" structure.
- Repeating the reader's own conclusion back to them.

> Too polished: *The weight of the moment settled over them, a quiet reminder that some wounds never truly heal.*
>
> In voice: *Nobody said anything. Kei poured the tea anyway.*

---

## 6. Rating

All ages up to R. Violence, dread, grief, swearing, romance and desire are all fine at that level. Nothing M-rated: no explicit sex. Fade to black is fine.

Heroines with their own goals, female leads and yuri are very welcome. Consent is never ambiguous on the page. Romance works best when it's tied to who the characters are and what they're going through.

---

## 7. Presentation

Never carry meaning by red versus green alone. Pair colour with shape, position, a label or an icon.

---

## 8. Before a scene ships

1. What does this scene promise the reader, and where does each promise get paid?
2. Did anyone win who didn't earn it?
3. With the names covered, can you tell who's speaking?
4. Is anyone lecturing? Is anyone a strawman?
5. Is there an em dash, or anything from the list in §5?
6. Does the sincere moment stay plain?
7. Run `shiori check`, and `shiori explore` for anything that branches.
